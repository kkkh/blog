At the end of June the watch recorded three rides for me: 57.14 kilometres on the 24th, 60.26 on the 25th, 55.10 on the 28th. That comes to 172.5 kilometres, all of it in Taiyuan, all of it along the Fen River.

None of the numbers below are from memory. I copied them out of the workout records, one line at a time[^1].

Moving time, elapsed time, climbing, and the temperature and humidity of each day are spread through the sections below and gathered in the fold at the end. The table was getting too wide to read; four rows could not carry that much.

The lane first, so the rest makes sense.

## The Lane Itself

The riverside cycleway runs down both banks of the Fen, laid out in two parallel strips. It starts in the north at the Shanglan ford bridge over the Fen, and its southern end is two kilometres past Yingbin Bridge. It opened on 1 May 2021, and the length announced that year was 75 kilometres; the two ends have been extended since, and the official figure quoted now is 85 kilometres[^2].

Each lane is five metres wide, five to eight on some stretches, with two metres of planting on either side. The surface is not asphalt but coloured ceramic granules — hard-wearing, bright, waterproof — which reads reddish in flat light and pales a shade under a hard sun[^3]. Along the whole route there are more than thirty entrances and exits, most of them joining the bridges across the Fen and the footbridges along the east and west riverside roads; eleven viewing and rest platforms; fifty-two bicycle assist systems for pushing a bike up; and eight hundred and seventy-seven street lamps across both banks[^4].

The rules are posted at the main entrances, and they are simple enough: the lane is for non-assisted bicycles only, with electric bikes and every other kind of vehicle banned; keep right and never ride against the flow; fifteen kilometres an hour is the ceiling; riders must be at least twelve, and under eighteen only with an adult; no passengers[^5]. I read the sign at the entrance almost every time, mostly to check that I was on the right lane.

## 24 June (Monday): 57.14 Kilometres, 6 Hours 41 Minutes of Riding

Away at 11\:45, done at 21\:13. The record says 6 hours 41 minutes 42 seconds of moving time, but the elapsed time is 9 hours 27 minutes 41 seconds — two hours forty-six minutes of that was standing still: junctions, red lights, buying water, getting out of the sun, and one stretch of sitting by the river where I lost track of how long I sat.

The watch logged 25°C and 28% humidity. That day Taiyuan’s high was 29°C and the low 15°C, so the morning was still pleasant; after noon there was nothing left but the sun.

The 257 metres of climbing is the most of the three days. There is no real hill to speak of in Taiyuan; almost all of that height came from footbridges, bridge approaches and the few road interchanges on the way — a flat stretch, then a ten-odd-metre ramp, stacked up until it totalled this.

An average of 8.5 kilometres an hour sounds slow. But this city has more traffic lights than road. The truth of riding in town is that no matter how hard you push, you cannot outrun the next junction.

## 25 June (Tuesday): 60.26 Kilometres, Barely a Stop

The interesting thing about this one is the gap between two numbers: 6 hours 38 minutes 04 seconds of riding, 6 hours 40 minutes 47 seconds elapsed. A difference of two minutes forty-three seconds. From leaving at 14\:02 to getting home at 20\:43, I was on the bike nearly the entire six and a half hours and stopped for under three minutes in total.

60.26 kilometres is the longest of the three, at 9.0 km/h and an average heart rate of 124, two beats below the previous day. Climbing: 193 metres. It was 30°C with humidity down at 22%, a dry heat in which sweat evaporates as it appears. It did not feel bad. It just meant drinking very fast, one bottle after another.

What hurt by the end was not the legs but the lower back and the palms. However thick the padding, six and a half hours without leaving the saddle means your backside has to work it out for itself.

## 28 June (Friday): 55.10 Kilometres, the Fastest Day

Away at 14\:27, home at 18\:30, four hours and three minutes. The clock and the workout time almost coincide on this one — there was no long stop anywhere in it. I worked it out myself from 14\:27 to 18\:30, which comes to exactly 4 hours 03 minutes, and that matches the 4\:03\:26 in the record.

All three extremes of the three days land on this ride: ==13.5 km/h, the fastest average; 73 metres of climbing, the least; 146 beats per minute, the highest.== A flat route and a rider pushing hard, added together, look like that.

It was also the hottest day of that week. In the weather service’s review of 24 to 30 June, the week averaged 25.4°C, and the extreme high of 35.3°C fell on the 28th. The watch logged 32°C when I set off. Half past two in the afternoon is the worst possible time of day to ride a bicycle, and I chose it anyway[^6].

The same river, the same direction, and three distances within five kilometres of each other. Split apart, they are three quite different ways of riding: on the 24th I drifted and stopped; on the 25th I simply travelled; on the 28th I pushed.

So 172.5 as a total does not say much. The two figures that do are how much I stopped and how much I climbed. [Note]{.label .warning}The more stopping, the lower the average; the less climbing, the closer the route stayed to the water.

## The Stretch by the Water Is the Reason for This Lane

The best part of the cycleway is not in the data.

Ride down to the river and there is water on one side and city on the other, the two lanes running parallel, east bank and west. Through the urban stretch of the Fen, the channel is fifty metres across at its narrowest and up to three hundred at its widest, divided into twelve impounded levels by four weirs, seven rubber dams and two sluice gates, with twenty-four bridges of various kinds crossing it[^7]. Riders do not need to hold any of that in mind. You just take it level by level, the water sometimes right beside you and sometimes well below.

After half past six, with the sun down, the wind comes off the water and the air cools earlier here than in the streets. This lane is busy in the evening: eight hundred and seventy-seven lamps lit all the way along, and every few hundred metres someone cycling, running, or out for a walk. On the 24th I did not finish until after nine at night, and most of that last hour and a bit was spent drifting along the river — it was cool there; the ride back into the city was the uncomfortable part.

Further up, the Fen River Scenic Area runs from the Shanglan ford bridge in the north to two kilometres past Yingbin Bridge in the south, forty-three kilometres in all, about twenty square kilometres in total; Victory Bridge divides it, with seventeen kilometres of natural wetland to the north and twenty-six kilometres of impounded landscape water to the south[^7]. I never reached the end of the wetland stretch. I only went back and forth between bridges.

## What Is Left on the Map

Eighty-five kilometres is the two lanes added together, from the ford bridge at Shanglan in the north to two kilometres past Yingbin Bridge in the south.

My three traces are each a long north–south line with a few side loops through the city attached. Which means 172.5 kilometres over three days is not the same as riding this lane end to end: west bank down, east bank back, and bridges, detours and the way home on top of that. Half the distance was spent getting to the river and getting away from it.

From one of the bridges I stopped on, I looked down at the lane: a bright thin line pasted along the water, with no visible end to the north and none to the south. The rest of it is out there. Some day I will start at the northern end and ride to the southern one, and not get off on the way.

## At the End

A ride log turns into a list of numbers very easily. 57.14, 60.26, 55.10 do not look like much in a table — averages between eight and thirteen kilometres an hour, which for cycling is a little embarrassing.

But I did go out on all three days and come back on all three days. I came back with sore legs, empty bottles and wet clothes.

What stays is something else. Around four o’clock on the 28th, on a stretch of riverbank with no trees, with the sun straight overhead and the road surface glaring, I put my head down and pedalled for a quarter of an hour. Not one figure was recorded for that stretch.
