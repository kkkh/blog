## 1. Why Read It

I began it because the opening line is quoted so much. I wanted to know what actually follows a sentence everyone can recite.

It was a summer, and I read it as a physical book, slowly. The first few dozen pages tripped me up: I would be moving along Anna’s line, then veer off into the countryside, into a man named Levin arguing about farming and worrying over the harvest. At one point I wanted to skip those chapters and keep only Anna’s.

What changed my mind was realising, somewhere in the middle, a simple fact: nearly half of this book contains almost no Anna. Tolstoy is not wandering; he does it on purpose — he sets two plots side by side so each explains the other. From that moment I was finally reading the novel.

## 2. The Skeleton First

:::info
Anna Karenina was begun in 1873 and serialised in The Russian Messenger from 1875 to 1877, in eight parts. It carries two nearly equal main lines: Anna and Vronsky on one, Levin and Kitty on the other. The two sets meet only rarely and otherwise live their separate lives. By report, late in the serialisation the editor disliked the content of the final part, and publishing its ending caused friction with the magazine — a detail often used as a footnote to the book itself[^1]. The eight-part division and the chapter counts vary a little from one edition to the next.
:::

For a book this thick I keep three maps, so that I don’t lock onto a single line:

| Approach | What it tracks | What it solves |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| By storyline | The turning points of each plot | Which chapter belongs to whose story |
| By character | Who is watching, who is watched | How opinion pushes the plot along |
| By scene | Ball, race, station, mowing | Anchoring the themes in concrete scenes |

+++primary Expand: where the weight of the eight parts falls (my own annotations)
- **Part 1**: both lines start at once — Anna goes to reconcile her brother, Levin comes to town to propose
- **Part 2**: the ball and the race; the first crack appears in Anna’s position
- **Part 3**: Levin works the fields; Anna is caught between two homes
- **Part 4**: each line driven to a crisis — one wants to leave, one wants to marry
- **Part 5**: Anna’s marriage breaks; Levin’s family is just beginning
- **Part 6**: country life; the fatigue between Anna and Vronsky surfaces
- **Part 7**: Anna’s collapse; the darkest part of the book
- **Part 8**: Levin’s problem of faith, and whether he will go as a volunteer
+++

One caution: the book is most easily read as one of two stories — the fate of a “bad woman”, or a love ground down by society. Both thin it out. What it really watches is how a person lives in other people’s eyes and how a person lives inside their own mind, and Tolstoy gives each of those its own line.

## 3. Passages and Marginalia

### On “family”: a line quoted until worn smooth

> *My own working translation:* Happy families are all alike; every unhappy family is unhappy in its own way.
>
> — Anna Karenina, Part 1, opening

==This is usually taken for a sigh about happiness, but it is really about the shape of the thing called a family: it succeeds in only one form, and fails in as many ways as there are families.==

My note: copy it onto a quote card and you waste it. It is closer to a definition the author lays down for the whole book — what he writes next is precisely one of those “unhappy in its own way” cases. Set a general rule at the start, then fill it with an entire novel: that is Tolstoy’s usual move[^2].

### On “being seen”: the plot is pushed by other people’s eyes

> *My own working translation:* She sits in company and those around her quietly step half a pace away; no one says anything, and yet she knows she has been set apart. What hurts is not being accused but being seen.
>
> — from the drift of Anna’s position, Parts 5 and 7

[危险]{.label .danger}==Almost every turn on Anna’s line is taken by other people’s eyes, not by her own feet.== The book rarely contains an explicit verdict, and yet the punishment is carried out by “not looking at her”.

My note: Tolstoy writes society the way he writes weather — no comment, only temperature and wind. Reading on, I saw that Anna’s true opponent is not any single person but the combined gaze of everyone.

### On “labour”: the brightest passage on Levin’s line

> *My own working translation:* He mows alongside the peasants, from early morning until the sun leans west. At the point of exhaustion his mind empties instead, leaving only the rise and fall of his arms; that happiness of thinking nothing at all he would not find again in many places afterwards.
>
> — from the drift of Levin’s mowing, Part 3

==On Levin’s line the subject is not really love; it is how to keep living a day at a time.==

My note: on a first reading I took these chapters for interludes and skipped them without regret; on a second I saw they are the other face of the same coin as Anna’s line. One person presses himself down into the ground through work, the other suspends herself in the air through love — and the difference in how each ends is already buried here.

:::success
**Read the two lines side by side**

The most important decision in reading Anna Karenina is not to skip Levin’s chapters. Set the two lines side by side and you find Tolstoy asking one question — how should a person live — with two answers: one going into society, one going into the land. Read only one line and the question is only half there.
:::

### On “living”: the answer Levin finds at the end

> *My own working translation:* He searches and turns it over and finds no account that can convince his reason; in the end he admits that reason will not give him an answer. The one thing he can hold is this: to live by an unseen rule inside him, and to be a little better to each person around him, nothing more.
>
> — from the drift of Levin’s ending, Part 8

++着重点++{.dot}The Anna plot asks whether life has any meaning, and the Levin plot gives an answer that is barely an answer at all. ==Tolstoy’s skill lies exactly there: he offers no handsome conclusion, only something a man has just managed to hold.==

My note: this ending leaves many readers unsatisfied — too soft, too ordinary. But like the opening line, it is the author closing the book’s seams: happiness has no drama, unhappiness does. What Levin finds is not a truth but a posture that lets him keep living.

:::warning
**Don’t read it as a moral tale**

The laziest reading passes judgement for the book: Anna was wrong, so she came to that end; or Anna was innocent and society destroyed her. Tolstoy himself does not do this — he gives Anna no verdict, and writes her to the last with a measure of tenderness. Read as a tale of reward and punishment, the passages of real craft get lost: what he writes is how each person struggles inside their own situation, not who deserves to be punished.
:::

## 4. What I Actually Use

1. **Read the two lines side by side; skip not one of Levin’s chapters.** Skip them and the remaining half looks like nothing but an affair; restore the other half and it turns back into a book about how to live.
2. **On Anna’s line, watch who is looking at her.** Nearly every shift in her position happens first in other people’s eyes and only then lands on her. Watching those gazes is more useful than watching the plot.
3. **Read the ending twice.** The first time you will find it soft; the second time you see that what Levin holds, small as it is, is the only thing in the book that stands — and Tolstoy would rather give that than a handsome summary.

## 5. Closing

Anna Karenina made me put one habit away: that a novel has to take sides. Tolstoy sets two utterly different roads beside each other, walks them both to the end, and then tells the reader, in a nearly plain way, that what lets a person keep going is usually nothing very grand.

Next: the Divine Comedy — Dante arranges an entire universe into a building with levels, and where a person goes has been drawn up in advance. I want to see how it reads when meaning is not sought but assigned.
