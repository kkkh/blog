## 1. Why I Read It

The first time, in a university library, I gave up three chapters in, beaten by the names — José Arcadio and Aureliano surfacing again and again. I drew a family tree from the cast list at the back, lost it by the third generation, and took the book back.

The second time was winter. One afternoon I opened to the first three pages: “Many years later, as he faced the firing squad, Colonel Aureliano Buendía was to remember...” The tense stopped me; I read the line twice, then read the whole book in one sitting.

Only afterwards did I see that the repeated names are not a threshold but a clue. García Márquez is not being lazy: one name carries one temperament, and one temperament leads to one kind of end. Readers get lost precisely because he wrote the state of being trapped into the naming rule itself.

## 2. The Skeleton First

:::info
One Hundred Years of Solitude was published in 1967, the best known of García Márquez’s novels. It runs to twenty unnumbered chapters and tells the rise and fall, over a hundred years, of the town of Macondo and seven generations of the Buendía family. The first authorised Chinese edition, translated by Fan Ye, appeared in 2011; before that, most Chinese versions in circulation were unauthorised.
:::

If the names keep tripping you up, you are missing a name map. I laid the seven generations out as a list:

| Generation | The recurring names | How it ends |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| First | José Arcadio, Úrsula | Found Macondo; he ends his days tied to the chestnut tree |
| Second | Aureliano (the Colonel), José Arcadio, Amaranta | The Colonel fights thirty-two wars; brother and sister miss each other for life |
| Third | Arcadio, Aureliano José | One dies before a firing squad, one by violence |
| Fourth | Remedios the Beauty; the twins Aureliano Segundo and José Arcadio Segundo | One ascends to heaven, two run the family fortune into the ground |
| Fifth | José Arcadio, Meme, Amaranta Úrsula | Running away, an affair, and a return to Macondo |
| Sixth | Aureliano Babilonia | Falls in love with his aunt, deciphers the parchment |
| Seventh | The child with a pig’s tail | Eaten by the ants, and the book closes |

+++primary Expand: two lines of temperament, buried in the names (my own annotations)
- **Anyone called José Arcadio**: impulsive, strong, takes the open road; a life pushing outward and a life hitting walls
- **Anyone called Aureliano**: solitary, stubborn, fixated on one thing — war, gold coins, the parchment; their solitude collapses inward
- **Anyone called Amaranta**: bound up with love that never returns, honey with poison in it
- **Úrsula**: the only clear-eyed one, outliving a century and watching the family repeat every mistake
- **Remedios**: appears twice, and belongs to this world neither time — one dies young, one ascends
+++

One warning: do not read “solitude” as a lyrical word. In García Márquez’s hands it is a mechanism: once a person lives only on his own terms, he is bound to walk his forebears’ road again. The repetition of names is the switch that starts it.

## 3. Passages and Marginalia

### On the opening: the first sentence tangles time

> “Many years later, as he faced the firing squad, Colonel Aureliano Buendía was to remember that distant afternoon when his father took him to discover ice.”
>
> — Chapter 1 (Gregory Rabassa’s translation)

==One sentence welds three times together:== the present (facing the firing squad), a future (many years later) and a past (that distant afternoon of the ice). Read it and you are already inside Macondo’s circular time.

[笔记]{.label .primary}The ice is a keepsake the book returns to again and again. At first it is the father’s expedition; later it is proof of the Colonel’s childhood; last it is one of Macondo’s lost relics. **One image, recycled** — that is the novel’s real stitching.

### On naming: the world was so recent

> “The world was so recent that many things lacked names, and in order to indicate them it was necessary to point.”
>
> — Chapter 1 (Gregory Rabassa’s translation)

[危险]{.label .danger}==A line that sounds romantic is in fact the book’s working principle.== If things have no names, you point at them; once they have names, people forget what they were pointing at.

++着重点++{.dot}There is a famous plague of insomnia in the book: the whole town cannot sleep, then begins to forget, so they label everything — “This is a table,” “This is a cow.” **Once names drain away, even daily life becomes impossible.** This passage explains the whole novel: why the Buendía names come round again. A name is the anchor against forgetting.

### On solitude: a telegram of five words

> “It’s raining in Macondo.”
>
> — telegram from Colonel Gerineldo Márquez to Colonel Aureliano Buendía
>
> “It’s August. Rain is normal.”
>
> — the reply
>
> *My own working translation.*

==A telegram asking for comfort is fended off with a weather report.== Gerineldo wanted “I miss you too”; he got a meteorological bulletin.

[笔记]{.label .primary}This is the book’s most economical statement of solitude: however close two people are, they cannot hear what the other is saying. The Colonel fights thirty-two wars, and each one leaves fewer men and shorter sentences. In the end he is not unwilling to speak; he has simply lost the trick of it.

### On the ending: the parchment and the last sentence

> “The races condemned to one hundred years of solitude did not have a second opportunity on earth.”
>
> — the last chapter (Gregory Rabassa’s translation)

==The book’s final line, and the motto García Márquez leaves on it.== It fastens repetition and ending together at once: Macondo’s history will happen only once, and so will this one generation of Buendías.

[笔记]{.label .primary}Only on the last page do you see that seven generations of repetition existed to make this line true: **if everything can happen only once, then repetition was the experiment the book ran on solitude’s behalf.**

:::success
**Does the label “magical realism” fit?**

Two camps have argued for a long time. One reads it as a political parable: the banana-company massacre, the endless civil wars, the town wiped from the map, all pointing at Latin American history. The other says its wildest events — the ascent to heaven, the flying carpet, the child with a pig’s tail — are narrated as calmly as a family chronicle, which shows that the wonder is simply how family memory tells itself. My reading is that both hold, but do not sell the book on the “magic”: that calm, which recounts miracles like household news, is the real feat.
:::

:::warning
**One trap in translations**

The translation history of this novel in Chinese is unusual. Before 2011, almost every version in circulation was unauthorised, relayed from English or Spanish, and sometimes cut. In 2011 the publisher obtained the rights and Fan Ye retranslated it, which is what made a “definitive” version possible. So the copy you pick up at a second-hand stall may have passed through any number of hands. Even among legitimate versions the names differ widely — Aureliano, Remedios and Úrsula all vary. When choosing, first check whether the names stay consistent from cover to cover, then whether the family relations are made clear.
:::

## 4. Three Reading Habits That Actually Stuck

1. **Draw your own family tree, in two columns.** One column for every José Arcadio, one for every Aureliano. When it is done you will see that the fates named in the title were written into that contrast from the start.
2. **Treat “it happened again” as a signal.** Each time something happens a second time, stop and find the first. Repetition is not the author rambling; it is this book’s grammar.
3. **Remember the temperaments, not the names.** You do not need to memorise seven generations. Just hold on to this: the José Arcadios push out, the Aurelianos fold in. Grip that, and the names turn from obstacles into signposts.

## 5. Closing

Finishing it, what I gained was not the word “magic” but a patience with repetition. I used to think the worst thing in a life was having to start over; now I half understand García Márquez: worse than starting over is walking the old road again and again without noticing.

Next: the Republic. Again a book about people building a city and agreeing how to live — one city imagined in a tropical rainforest, the other argued into being in a dialogue. I want to see which city survives questioning better.

[^1]: Macondo has a real model: García Márquez was born in Aracataca, a town in Colombia, and his grandparents’ house is the basis for the big house in the novel; the “banana company” corresponds to the real suppression of a local strike in 1928. He later said it was not he who invented the magic — Latin American life had always looked like that.
