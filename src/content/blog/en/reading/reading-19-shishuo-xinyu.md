## 1. Why I Read It

The first time I opened Shishuo Xinyu I went in for “Wei-Jin refinement”, expecting ancients’ gossip. The first category, “Virtuous Conduct”, put me to sleep — pages of grave pronouncements, nothing like the free-spirited figures I imagined. I shelved it a long time.

What pulled me back was one entry in “Wilful Conduct”: Wang Ziyou, taken one night by a wish to see a friend, rowed for a whole night; at dawn he reached the man’s gate, then turned back, saying he had set out on a mood and the mood was spent. The odd thing about this book is that it never explains itself.

It skips even the step of arguing. Other old books hurry to hand down conclusions; this one records a single remark or gesture and files the person under a door. On this re-reading I carried one question: how can fragments with no beginning or end piece together an age? The answer narrowed to the thirty-six doors. The door’s name is the verdict, and the book’s position sits entirely in its table of contents.

## 2. The Skeleton First

:::info
The standard text of Shishuo Xinyu has thirty-six categories and about 1,130 entries (counts differ between editions), recording the words and deeds of notable men from the late Han to the Eastern Jin. The compilation is usually credited to Liu Yiqing, a prince of the Liu Song, though it likely came from retainers under him. It is neither official history nor, really, fiction. It reads like an archive filed by “standard of judgement”: set up thirty-six kinds of appraisal, then sort people into them.
:::

Anyone who cannot enter it is usually reading it as a storybook, page by page. Learn the names of the doors first, because the door names are the age’s standard of judgement:

| Common categories | What it really asks | What to watch for |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Virtuous Conduct | Is this person to be trusted? | The verdict is often in the onlookers’ reactions |
| Speech and Conversation | Is the remark well made? | A good line often is the whole entry |
| Broad-Mindedness, Uprightness | Is the bearing steady under pressure? | The quieter the reaction, the higher the score |
| Wilful Conduct, Arrogance | Does the way of life keep the rules? | The more it breaks them, the likelier it is praise |
| Extravagance, Infatuation | Where does a person come off the rails? | The category name itself is the verdict |

+++primary Expand: which of the thirty-six doors to open first (my own annotations)
- **Virtuous Conduct**: the first door, the highest standard, the gravest tone
- **Speech and Conversation, Affairs of State, Letters and Scholarship**: beside Virtuous Conduct, the old frame of Confucius’s “four subjects”
- **Uprightness, Broad-Mindedness, Insight, Appreciation and Praise, Ranking and Evaluation**: a slab of doors about judging people
- **Admonitions, Quick Perception, Early Wisdom, Uninhibited Boldness, Appearance and Bearing**: specific labels for temperament
- **Wilful Conduct, Arrogance, Mocking and Jesting, Slighting**: the notables’ tempers, the liveliest reading
- **Extravagance, Stinginess, Irascibility, Infatuation, Enmity**: the book’s later, “negative” cases
+++

One warning: do not take Shishuo Xinyu for reliable history. Many entries come from rumour and elaboration; the same event, filed under different doors, can be judged in opposite ways. It records not “what actually happened” but “how that age appraised people”.

## 3. Passages and Marginalia

### On “friendship”: four characters end a friendship

> 管宁、华歆共园中锄菜，见地有片金，管挥锄与瓦石不异，华捉而掷去之。又尝同席读书，有乘轩冕过门者，宁读如故，歆废书出看。宁割席分坐曰：子非吾友也。
>
> — Virtuous Conduct
>
> *My own working translation:* Guan Ning and Hua Xin were hoeing vegetables together when they turned up a piece of gold: Guan Ning hoed on as if it were a stone, while Hua Xin picked it up and threw it away. Sharing a mat to read, they saw a carriage of office pass the gate; Guan Ning read on, Hua Xin left the book to look. Guan Ning split the mat and sat apart, saying: You are not my friend.

==The whole weight presses on the last four characters: You are not my friend.== Guan Ning does not call Hua Xin greedy or a seeker of fame. He makes a decision on the spot: since the two disagree about what is worth a glance, they need not share a mat again.

My note: the entry is usually read as “Guan Ning lofty, Hua Xin worldly”. Yet the book files it under its first door, and what it praises is **judgement**, not fastidiousness — the ability to see, in two small motions, whether two people can get along at all.

### On “a way with words”: the grade of a single sentence

> 谢太傅寒雪日内集，与儿女讲论文义。俄而雪骤，公欣然曰：白雪纷纷何所似？兄子胡儿曰：撒盐空中差可拟。兄女曰：未若柳絮因风起。公大笑乐。
>
> — Speech and Conversation
>
> *My own working translation:* On a cold snowy day the Grand Tutor Xie gathered the household to discuss the meaning of texts. The snow came on suddenly, and he said with pleasure: What does falling snow resemble? His nephew Huer said: It might be compared to salt scattered in the air. His niece said: Not so good as willow catkins rising in the wind. The Grand Tutor laughed aloud.

++着重点++{.dot}The entry records three sentences, a scoring done on the spot. “Salt scattered in the air” is not bad — the shape matches. But “willow catkins rising in the wind” brings weight into it: snow does not come down grain by grain, it drifts. Xie An’s laugh is the verdict of that age.

My note: giving an entry’s whole weight to one sentence is the book’s commonest move. It does not say “this girl is very talented”; it leaves only the willow-catkin line, and lets the reader weigh the rest.

### On “composure”: holding the feeling down as a craft

> 谢公与人围棋，俄而谢玄淮上信至。看书竟，默然无言，徐向局。客问淮上利害？答曰：小儿辈大破贼。意色举止，不异于常。
>
> — Broad-Mindedness
>
> *My own working translation:* The Duke of Xie was playing go when word arrived from Xie Xuan on the Huai River. He read the letter, said nothing, and slowly turned back to the board. His guest asked how matters stood. He answered: My little ones have made a great rout of the rebels. His expression and manner were no different from usual.

:::success
This entry is filed under “Broad-Mindedness”, praising the skill of **holding a huge event down without letting it show**. The letter reports the victory at the Fei River; he reads it and goes on placing stones, answering in a single line. What is telling is that the same affair leaves a tail elsewhere, in the Book of Jin: after his guest left he went inside, and crossing the threshold broke the teeth of his wooden clogs. That loss of composure is what the entry truly wants you to savour.
:::

### On “a mood”: a whole night’s journey, then turning back at the gate

> 王子猷居山阴，夜大雪，眠觉，开室，命酌酒，四望皎然。因起彷徨，咏左思《招隐诗》。忽忆戴安道。时戴在剡，即便夜乘小船就之。经宿方至，造门不前而返。人问其故，王曰：吾本乘兴而行，兴尽而返，何必见戴？
>
> — Wilful Conduct
>
> *My own working translation:* Wang Ziyou was living in Shanyin. One night heavy snow fell; he woke, opened the room, had wine brought, and looked out on brightness. He paced, reciting Zuo Si’s “Poem of Summoning the Recluse”, and suddenly remembered Dai Andao. Dai was then in Shan, so at night he took a small boat to him. A whole night later he arrived, yet at the gate he turned back. Asked why, Wang said: I came on a mood and the mood is spent, so I return — why must I have seen Dai?

[危险]{.label .danger}==Notice it is filed under “Wilful Conduct”, not “Broad-Mindedness”.== The same night’s behaviour, under different doors, means different things: under Broad-Mindedness it is steadiness, under Wilful Conduct it is waywardness. The compiler puts it under “Wilful Conduct”, and what is praised is the self-consistent logic — he never pretends he went to see anyone.

My note: only here did I understand how to read this book. It does not judge whether an act is good or bad; it judges which **kind** it belongs to. Whether Wang Ziyou’s night was worth it depends on which door it falls behind.

### On “farewell”: weeping in the way that knew the other best

> 顾彦先平生好琴，及丧，家人常以琴置灵床上。张季鹰往哭之，不胜其恸，遂径上床，鼓琴，作数曲竟，抚琴曰：顾彦先颇复赏此不？因又大恸，遂不执孝子手而出。
>
> — Grief for the Departed
>
> *My own working translation:* Gu Yanxian had loved the lute all his life. When he died, his family kept the lute on his spirit bed. Zhang Jiying came to weep for him; grief overwhelmed him, so he went up to the bed and played several pieces, then laid his hand on the lute and said: Yanxian, can you still appreciate this? Then he broke down again, and left without taking the hand of the filial son.

==The heaviest words are the last: he left without taking the hand of the filial son.== By ritual a mourner grips the son’s hand before leaving; he does not — not from rudeness, but because he wept past ritual. It is filed under “Grief for the Departed”, and what it praises is not that he knew the rules but that he did not finish walking them.

My note: the book’s best moments almost always sit at the tail of a gesture. Pouring wine, playing go, splitting a mat, playing the lute — the gesture finishes, the person stands up, and the author says nothing more.

:::warning
**One thing to remember before you start**

The main text is often too short to understand: a bare “You are not my friend”, with who and why left to context. So never read a bare text. The real key is **Liu Xiaobiao’s commentary** — it fills in a figure’s family, offices and antecedents, and many entries cannot be told apart as praise or blame without it[^1].
:::

## 4. Lessons and Practice

1. **Read the door name first, then the story.** Given an entry, see which door it hangs under: praise in “Broad-Mindedness”, blame in “Irascibility” — the same act can be read two ways. Once that is second nature, you ask it of people too: which category does this belong to?
2. **Follow one person across the book.** String together the entries on Xie An or Wang Ziyou scattered through the doors — front view, back view, loss of composure. Pieced together they are fuller than any formal biography: the book does not write lives whole, it distributes a life across categories.
3. **Copy sentences, not morals.** The book is thick with good lines, one sentence worth an essay. “Not so good as willow catkins rising in the wind”, “the mood is spent, so I return — why must I have seen Dai”: write them out and say them aloud, and they do more than a heap of admonitions.

## 5. Closing

Closing the book, I dropped an earlier view: I used to think Shishuo Xinyu was a heap of loose sand. Now I think of it as rows of labelled drawers, into which each age drops the person it valued most. It gives no conclusion because it trusts that a few more entries will grow a judgement in you.

Next: Faust. A book exactly its opposite — Shishuo Xinyu records one person in a single line; Faust questions one life across a whole verse drama.

[^1]: The commentator Liu Xiaobiao lived barely a century after the book’s figures, and the family histories, offices and anecdotes he adds are reliable; later readers can hardly do without him. Some find the commentary too heavy, drowning the main text — but the “brief” main text and the “full” commentary are two sides of one thing: the brevity is the tone of the age, the fullness a later reconstruction.
[^2]: The frame of “thirty-six doors” is not seamless: the opening Virtuous Conduct, Speech and Conversation, Affairs of State and Letters and Scholarship borrow the old names of Confucius’s four subjects, and the later doors run on by kind of appraisal with no strict logical progression — more like the result of long accumulation and repeated sorting.
