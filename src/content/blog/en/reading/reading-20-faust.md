## 1. Why I Read It

I first read Faust for the “deal with the devil”, expecting a story of a soul sold. A few pages in, I surrendered: it is a verse drama, in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century German, with several translations in between, and it read haltingly.

What made me pick it up again was a concrete fact: Goethe worked on this book for nearly sixty years. He began in his twenties and kept at it until shortly before his death in his eighties; Part Two was published only after he died. One man spending almost a whole life on another man’s life carries weight by itself.

This time the first detail I noted was neither the devil nor the love story but the translation at the start: Faust, in his study, translating the Gospel of John, rewrites “In the beginning was the Word” four times over and lands on “In the beginning was the Deed”. Only then did I realise that the first scene of this great drama is really about how a single sentence should be translated.

## 2. The Skeleton First

:::info
Faust is Goethe’s verse drama, written across nearly sixty years. Part One appeared in 1808; Part Two only after Goethe’s death in 1832. More than twenty years separate them, and they read almost like two works: Part One is concentrated and hot, circling the study, the town and the tragedy of Gretchen; Part Two is ornate and symbolic, moving through the imperial court, myth and Greece. The play is not there to “deliver a lesson”; it lets one man err again and again, and rise again and again.
:::

Telling the two parts apart is the easiest way into the book:

| | Part One | Part Two |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Published | 1808 | 1832, after the author’s death |
| Setting | Study, town, prison | Court, myth, Greece |
| Companions | Mephistopheles and Gretchen | Mephistopheles and Helen |
| Manner | Concentrated, hot | Elaborate, symbolic |
| Ending | Tragedy left on earth | Angels carry the soul away |

+++primary Expand: a work written across a whole life (my own annotations)
- **Around the 1770s**: the earliest “Urfaust” takes shape, the characters already there
- **1790**: part of it published as Faust, A Fragment
- **1808**: Part One properly published
- **The next twenty-odd years**: Part Two largely set aside, touched now and then
- **1832**: Part Two published after Goethe’s death, and the book closes
+++

One warning: do not read Faust as a moral figure. He is neither good nor bad; what the wager bets on is not whether he will fall but whether he will **stop**. He is allowed to err, so long as he keeps pushing forward.

## 3. Passages and Marginalia

### On “the beginning”: rewriting “In the beginning was the Word” four times

> Geschrieben steht: »Im Anfang war das Wort!«
> Hier stock ich schon! Wer hilft mir weiter fort?
> Ich kann das Wort so hoch unmöglich schätzen,
> Ich muß es anders übersetzen,
> Wenn ich vom Geiste recht erleuchtet bin.
> Geschrieben steht: Im Anfang war der Sinn.
> Bedenke wohl die erste Zeile,
> Daß deine Feder sich nicht übereile!
> Ist es der Sinn, der alles wirkt und schafft?
> Es sollte stehn: Im Anfang war die Kraft!
> Doch, auch indem ich dieses niederschreibe,
> Schon warnt mich was, daß ich dabei nicht bleibe.
> Mir hilft der Geist! Auf einmal seh ich Rat
> Und schreibe getrost: Im Anfang war die Tat!
>
> — Part One, “Study”
>
> *My own working translation:* It is written: “In the beginning was the Word!” Here I already falter — who will help me on? I cannot prize the Word so highly; I must translate it otherwise, if I am rightly enlightened by the spirit. It is written: In the beginning was the Sense. Consider well the first line, that your pen do not rush. Is it the Sense that works and creates all things? It should read: In the beginning was the Power! Yet even as I write this down, something warns me I will not stay with it. The spirit helps me! Suddenly I see the way, and write boldly: In the beginning was the Deed!

==These dozen lines are the first scene of the book and its crux.== Wort, Sinn, Kraft, Tat — each sinks a step lower, all the way from “saying” down to “doing”. Faust is not fussing over wording; he is finding a position he can stand on.

++着重点++{.dot}Goethe turns a sentence translated for a thousand years into a stage, and lets the translator change his mind in public. The whole play’s direction presses into these four revisions: **“Word” is too still; only “Deed” matches the restlessness he feels right now.**

### On “the wager”: one sentence decides it

> Werd ich zum Augenblicke sagen:
> Verweile doch! du bist so schön!
> Dann magst du mich in Fesseln schlagen,
> Dann will ich gern zugrunde gehn!
>
> — Part One, “Study”
>
> *My own working translation:* If ever I say to the moment: Stay a while, you are so fair! Then you may clap me in chains, then I will gladly go to my ruin!

[危险]{.label .danger}==This is the most famous line in the book, and the clause of a contract.== His confidence comes precisely from his restlessness: Mephistopheles bets he can be settled, Faust bets he never will be. Note the phrasing — “if ever I should say” — the moment has not arrived; he is asking for a future one.

My note: in Part Two he does say it, but as “I might say” — a moment he does not yet own, only glimpsed in imagination. Whether the wager is won keeps the whole book busy until the last page.

### On “two souls”: one man split in two

> Zwei Seelen wohnen, ach! in meiner Brust,
> Die eine will sich von der andern trennen;
> Die eine hält, in derber Liebeslust,
> Sich an die Welt mit klammernden Organen;
> Die andere hebt gewaltsam sich vom Dust
> Zu den Gefilden hoher Ahnen.
>
> — Part One, “Before the Gate”
>
> *My own working translation:* Two souls, alas, dwell within my breast, and one would part from the other; the one clings to the world with clutching organs, in crude delight of love; the other lifts itself by force from the dust toward the fields of high ancestors.

==“Two souls” is not a figure of speech but the mechanics of the book.== Every episode — study, wager, love, power — can be read as these two forces pulling against each other. He is most human when he sinks; he is most like what he wants to become when he climbs.

My note: reading this, I thought of the translation at the start. Wort is the upward half, Tat the downward half; in the very first scene he has already chosen where the tension will come from.

### On “theory”: an oft-quoted piece of mockery

> Grau ist alle Theorie,
> Und grün des Lebens goldner Baum.
>
> — Part One, “Study” (Mephistopheles to the student who comes to enrol)
>
> *My own working translation:* Grey is all theory, and green the golden tree of life.

:::success
These two lines are often quoted as encouragement, yet the speaker is **Mephistopheles**, and the tone is double-edged: it sounds like advice against book-learning, and also like an excuse for not thinking. Put it back in place and Goethe looks far less naive — he has the devil say a truth, and leaves you to weigh it.
:::

### On “salvation”: two lines at the close

> Wer immer strebend sich bemüht,
> Den können wir erlösen.
>
> — Part Two, chorus of angels
>
> *My own working translation:* Whoever strives with all his might, him we can save.

> Das Ewig-Weibliche
> Zieht uns hinan.
>
> — Part Two, closing lines
>
> *My own working translation:* The eternal feminine draws us upward.

==The first is the verdict, the second the elegy.== The wager runs across two books, and in the end it is not settled by anything magnificent Faust does but by a sentence of judgement: as long as he still strives, he has not lost. That is not forgiveness so much as an acknowledgement of the process.

My note: only at the end did I feel the weight of that opening “In the beginning was the Deed”. The whole book is a gloss on the one word — moving a man from “what does he believe” to “what does he do”.

:::warning
**Two things to settle before you read**

One is language. In the German, the weight of Wort (word), Sinn (sense), Kraft (power), Tat (deed) is hard to carry over whole into a translation; keep the four senses in mind, or the translation scene will look baffling. The other is length. Do not expect to read Part Two straight through; it is more like a long masquerade, with figures and scenes changing constantly, and it comes clearer if you stop every so often[^1].
:::

## 4. Lessons and Practice

1. **Convert “what to believe” into “what to do”.** The point Faust’s revision lands on is to bring abstract belief back down to concrete action. My own rough rule: when something is unclear, take the smallest step first; the answer usually shows up after you start moving.
2. **Recognise the two souls in your chest.** The half that wants ease and the half that wants distance are often both present. Do not rush to kill one off; acknowledge both, then ask which half gets to take one more step this week. That costs less than forcing yourself to be positive.
3. **Be wary of “grey theory” — and of whoever says it.** The famous line comes from the devil’s mouth. Whenever a voice tells you not to overthink, first see what the speaker wants you to go and do.

## 5. Closing

Closing the book, I dropped my first prejudice — that it was a legend of a soul sold. It is nearer a long experiment, and what it tests is whether one man can keep erring and still move toward the light. Goethe gives him no clean ending, only a fair sentence: while you still strive, there is still room.

Next: the Iliad. Faust follows the inner order of a single life; that one is the wrath and honour of a single war, a wholly different noise.

[^1]: There is a textual point too: Part Two had no fixed text until after Goethe’s death, and scholars still debate the order and attribution of individual passages. Do not insist on the “one correct reading”; read for the story itself first.
[^2]: That translation scene has one more layer: the Gospel’s original is the Greek logos, which already carries several senses — word, reason, way. Goethe has Faust choose among them, so that the play meets, right at the start, the idea that meaning can be chosen.
