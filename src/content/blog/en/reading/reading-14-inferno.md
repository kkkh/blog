## 1. Why I Read It

The first time I picked up the Divine Comedy was in the autumn of 2024, from a brittle, borrowed copy of Wang Weike’s Chinese prose translation. Twice I stalled in the first canto: a dark wood, three beasts, a Roman poet appearing out of nowhere, the whole thing moving like a dream while every line ended in rhyme. Flattened into prose the rhythm vanished; I shut the book and filed it under “know the gist, no need to finish”.

What pulled me back was Ulysses. Digging around, I came across Canto XXVI: Dante’s Ulysses stands in a fissure of the eighth circle and calls out to his crew that they were not made to live as brutes. The eighth circle houses the fraudulent. A man Homer wrote as a hero, given one of the poem’s most exalted speeches by Dante himself — why is he here? Not lust, not wrath, but the one kind of sin that takes a mind to commit.

That was the day I finally saw the Comedy’s real architecture: it is not cataloguing misdeeds, it is ranking evil. The deeper you go, the more intelligence the sin requires, the more deliberate and less forgivable it becomes. What won me over was not that Dante wrote a Hell; it is that he dared to grade sin.

## 2. The Skeleton First

:::info
The Divine Comedy divides into Hell, Purgatory and Paradise, thirty-three cantos each, plus the canto that opens the whole poem — one hundred exactly. Inferno therefore has thirty-four. It is written in a metre Dante invented: three lines to a rhyme, each tercet interlocking with the next, called terza rima. The whole journey is set around Easter of the year 1300.
:::

The cheapest way into Inferno is to memorise, first, who lives in each of the nine circles, rather than starting with the allusions:

| Level | Who is kept here | How I read it |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Prologue | The dark wood and three beasts | The start is “lost”, not “wicked” |
| Gate and vestibule | The fence-sitters who never chose | Not taking a side is a recorded position too |
| Circle One, Limbo | Unbaptised sages and infants | Good people live here, not bad ones |
| Circles Two to Five | Lust, gluttony, avarice, wrath | The “incontinent”, driven by appetite |
| Circle Six | Heresy | Wrong in belief, not in act |
| Circle Seven | Violence, in three rings | Against others, against oneself, against God |
| Circle Eight | Fraud, in ten ditches | The deeper you go, the more brains the sin takes |
| Circle Nine | Treachery, in four zones | The bottom is reserved for betraying trust |
| The centre | Three-faced Lucifer | Three mouths, one sinner each |

+++primary Expand: how the nine circles divide (my own annotations)
- **Circle Two, lust**: Minos sits at the entrance, wrapping his tail to fix how many levels down a soul must fall
- **Circle Three, gluttony**: Cerberus guards the gate; the greedy lie in mud and rain
- **Circle Four, avarice**: hoarders and spenders shove weights into each other
- **Circle Five, wrath**: sunk in the marsh of Styx, the angry brawling on the surface, the sullen below
- **Circle Six, heresy**: in burning tombs, Farinata stands half-erect in his coffin, unbowed
- **Circle Seven, violence**: ring one against others, ring two against oneself (suicides become trees), ring three against God
- **Circle Eight, fraud**: ten ditches, from flatterers to counterfeiters, with Ulysses in the eighth
- **Circle Nine, treachery**: the frozen lake Cocytus, its four zones named for Cain, Antenor, Ptolemy, Judas
- **The centre**: Lucifer with three faces and three mouths, chewing Judas, Brutus and Cassius
+++

One caution: do not take “deeper is worse” as a moralist’s slogan. Dante has his own logic — incontinence (no self-control) is light, fraud (deliberately using other people) is heavy. He is not asking what frightens you; he is asking what you chose.

## 3. Passages and Marginalia

### On the starting point: the first line of middle age

> Nel mezzo del cammin di nostra vita
> mi ritrovai per una selva oscura,
> ché la diritta via era smarrita.
>
> — Inferno I, lines 1-3
>
> *My own working translation:* Midway upon the journey of our life I found myself in a dark wood, for the straight way was lost.

==This is the poem’s doorway, and its most honest line.== It gives no name and no identity, only a state: lost. Dante sets an equals sign between “the middle of life” and “gone astray”.

My note: the poem opens with no god and no judgement, just a man standing still and admitting he has lost the road. Lower the posture first, and the descent that follows can stand.

### On the gate: a door that only states consequences

> Per me si va ne la città dolente,
> per me si va ne l'etterno dolore,
> per me si va tra la perduta gente.
>
> — Inferno III, lines 1-3
>
> *My own working translation:* Through me you pass into the city of woe, through me you pass into eternal pain, through me you pass among the lost people.

:::success
**Read the inscription backwards and it makes sense**
Its final line is “Lasciate ogne speranza, voi ch’intrate” — abandon every hope, you who enter. It is usually quoted like a horror-film tagline, but it is really a **definition**: what makes Hell Hell is not the pain, it is that afterwards no other possibility is left.
:::

[危险]{.label .danger}==One door apart divides not comfort from suffering, but removes “hope” as a variable.== Nine words that define Hell outdo every torture scene that follows.

### On desire: how “love” lands people in Hell

> Amor, ch'a nullo amato amar perdona,
>
> — Inferno V, line 103
>
> *My own working translation:* Love, which permits no one who is loved to return no love.

++着重点++{.dot}Francesca and Paolo were reading a book when they fell for each other, and that day they read no further. Dante makes “the book” and “the desire” two sides of a single thing: what condemns them is not appetite alone, but appetite taught by a story.

My note: the strongest stroke in Canto V is that Dante the narrator faints after hearing Francesca. The only thing in Hell that costs the narrator his composure is not torture; it is a well-told love story. His point: if even he is carried off by such telling, what of anyone else?

### On guilt: a hero filed under fraud

> fatti non foste a viver come bruti,
> ma per seguir virtute e canoscenza.
>
> — Inferno XXVI, lines 119-120
>
> *My own working translation:* You were not made to live as brutes, but to pursue virtue and knowledge.

==This line is quoted so often that the context has been almost quoted away.== Dante puts the finest piece of humanity in Ulysses’ mouth, then locks him in the eighth circle.

My note: Ulysses uses the name of “virtue and knowledge” to urge his crew past the edge of the known world, and the ship goes down with all hands. Dante’s verdict is plain — idealism is no immunity, and deceiving people with a noble reason is still fraud. It also explains why, for Dante, the clever sin sits nearer the bottom than the muddled one.

### On the exit: after one night, the stars

> E quindi uscimmo a riveder le stelle.
>
> — Inferno XXXIV, line 139
>
> *My own working translation:* And thence we came forth, to see again the stars.

[笔记]{.label .primary}This is the last line of Inferno. All three parts — Hell, Purgatory, Paradise — close on “the stars”, a ring Dante left himself. Climbing from the bottom of Hell up to the sea of the other hemisphere, it is dawn: he puts the book’s darkest place right beside a sunrise.

:::warning
**The trap of a single translation**
Chinese renderings of the Comedy fall into roughly two lines: prose (Wang Weike, Tian Dewang), easy to read, good for getting the plot straight; and verse (Huang Guobin), which keeps the sound of terza rima but twists the syntax. I began with Wang Weike alone: smooth, but it never felt like a poem. Only when I set a prose version against a verse one, plot against sound, did I hear why the poem is built in threes and not fours. Keep at least two translations on the desk; never just one.
:::

## 4. What I Put to Use

1. **Read the map before the text.** Learn who lives in each circle first; when a stranger appears, ask which circle he is in and whether his sin is incontinence or fraud. The position is itself the commentary.
2. **Read a long poem aloud.** The Comedy is written for the ear; terza rima is almost inaudible when you read silently. Even without Italian, the joints surface if you read the marked rhymes aloud in your own language, and the sentence breaks come with them.
3. **Use Limbo as a mirror.** Circle One holds unbaptised sages — good men who nonetheless have no place in Heaven. That is the most modern circle of all: many people are not villains, they simply lack the condition for entry. Telling “evil” apart from “arrived too late” is what keeps the book from turning you into a moral judge.

## 5. Closing

Finishing Inferno changed an old picture for me: Hell is not there to frighten you, it is there to **sort** you. Dante spends thirty-four cantos arranging every human wrong into a staircase from light to heavy, and deliberately leaves the deepest steps for the cleverest, best-argued men. That unsettles more than any threat.

Next: the Wenxin Diaolong. Both books are about “writing” — one uses poetry to trace where the heart falls, the other uses parallel prose to explain how a piece of writing grows. I want to see how far those two minds are apart.

[^1]: The rhyme scheme of terza rima is ABA BCB CDC: the middle rhyme of one tercet becomes the opening rhyme of the next, so its breaks carry a natural hook — hear one line and you wait for the next.
[^2]: Dante was exiled from Florence in 1302 over politics and spent the rest of his life in exile; the Comedy was largely written during it. He filed both enemies and patrons into Hell and Heaven — the poem’s “judgement” was never neutral.
