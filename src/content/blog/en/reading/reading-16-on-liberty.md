## 1. Why I Read It

In the spring of 2025 I kept running into the same question in arguments online: over a private matter that harms no one, does anyone else have the standing to interfere? Some people reached for “public order and good morals”; others replied, “none of your business”. By the end nobody could say where the line of “none of your business” actually ran.

So I went back to Mill’s On Liberty. What drew me in first was not the content but an old Chinese title — Yan Fu had rendered it as Qunji Quanjie Lun, “On the Boundary of Rights between the Group and the Self”. The boundary between “group” and “self” is exactly the line I could not argue straight.

The book is only five chapters and more than a century old, yet it reads as if it were speaking straight into today’s arguments. Mill really answers one question: under what circumstances may society legitimately interfere with a person’s conduct? His answer is startlingly short — only when that conduct harms others.

## 2. The Skeleton First

:::info
On Liberty was published in 1859, the same year as Darwin’s Origin of Species. Five chapters. Mill worked it out with his wife, Harriet Taylor, who died a year before it went to press; the dedication at the front is written to her. At its centre is a single “harm principle”: the only purpose for which power may be rightfully exercised over a member of a community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, physical or moral, is not sufficient warrant.
:::

The five chapters can be taken in at a glance:

| Chapter | Title | What it settles |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| I | Introductory | Lays down the harm principle as the master rule |
| II | Of the Liberty of Thought and Discussion | Whether fear of harm is grounds for silencing anyone |
| III | Of Individuality, as One of the Elements of Well-Being | Why a person should live as himself, not as someone else |
| IV | Of the Limits to the Authority of Society over the Individual | Draws “harm” down into everyday conduct |
| V | Applications | Takes it to particular cases, and closes the book |

+++primary Expand: the three fields the harm principle marks out (my own annotations)
- **The self-regarding part**: a person’s own thought, tastes and way of life — society has no authority over it
- **The other-regarding part**: once another’s interests are damaged, society gains jurisdiction
- **The middle ground**: Mill admits it is hard to draw, but insists on the line first, the rules second
- **What he keeps stressing**: not “how grave is the harm”, but “has anyone actually been harmed”
- **The easily missed layer**: Mill also says a person can harm others by inaction
+++

One caution: “harm no one” does not mean “never make anyone uncomfortable”. Mill’s harm is damage to particular interests, to those interests that ought to count as rights — not offence, distaste, or “I just don’t like it”. Let “I don’t like it” count as harm and the whole principle runs backwards.

## 3. Passages and Marginalia

### On the definition of freedom: one line that closes the door

> The only freedom which deserves the name, is that of pursuing our own good in our own way, so long as we do not attempt to deprive others of theirs, or impede their efforts to obtain it.
>
> — On Liberty, ch. I

==Mill gives “freedom” no romantic definition; he gives it a boundary.== The first half is a right, the second half a condition, and only the two locked together are what he means by freedom.

My note: the line wears well because it makes freedom something you can execute: not “you may have whatever you want”, but “can your way and another’s way coexist”. Every claim to unlimited freedom has dropped the second half.

### On the harm principle: the master key of the book

> The sole end for which mankind are warranted, individually or collectively, in interfering with the liberty of action of any of their number, is self-protection. [...] His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant.
>
> — On Liberty, ch. I

:::success
**Why “it’s for your own good” is not a reason**
The blade of this sentence is all in the last half. Most paternal interference flies the flag of “your own good”, and Mill nails the road shut: a benefit cannot serve as a warrant. To govern another, first show that he has harmed another.
:::

[危险]{.label .danger}==Keeping “you shouldn’t do that” apart from “you may do that” is the whole strength of the book.==

### On speech: a hypothesis too extreme to leave room for exceptions

> If all mankind minus one, were of one opinion, and only one person were of the contrary opinion, mankind would be no more justified in silencing that one person, than he, if he had the power, would be justified in silencing mankind.
>
> — On Liberty, ch. II

==The hypothesis is absurdly impossible, and Mill uses it precisely to shut every exception.== If your reason is “everyone says so”, this sentence still applies.

My note: Mill adds two more practical arguments — the suppressed opinion may be true; and even if it is false, only testing it in argument lets the correct side actually understand itself. He turns free speech from a right into an instrument for keeping truth awake.

### On individuality: why a person should live as himself

> He who lets the world, or his own portion of it, choose his plan of life for him, has no need of any other faculty than the ape-like one of imitation.
>
> — On Liberty, ch. III

++着重点++{.dot}“The ape-like one of imitation” is no gentle comparison. What Mill is saying is not the platitude “have a mind of your own”, but: choose for yourself and you bring observation, reasoning and judgment into play; let others choose and those faculties never grow.

My note: Chapter III is often read as a matter of “character”, when it is really a matter of capacity. For Mill, individuality is not decoration; it is the condition under which a person’s faculties get exercised at all. Without it, people become easier to govern — and weaker.

### On the state: why the last line is not about institutions

> The worth of a State, in the long run, is the worth of the individuals composing it.
>
> — On Liberty, ch. V

[笔记]{.label .primary}The book is about individual freedom, and it ends on the state. Mill’s point runs backwards: how far a country can get depends on what it does not crush its individuals into.

My note: he goes on to say that a state which dwarfs its people to make them docile will find that “with small men no great thing can really be accomplished”. The line joins “freedom” to “national power” — he is not merely speaking up for the individual, he is doing the sums.

:::warning
**The trap of reading the translation against the original**
There is one ditch in Chinese renderings of this book: the word harm. Mill’s harm means damage to particular interests — even to those interests “which ought to be considered as rights” — narrower than the everyday “discomfort” and heavier than mere offence. Chinese versions waver between “伤害” and “危害”, and the moment harm is widened into “offence”, the whole principle is emptied out. Yan Fu’s old rendering, Qunji Quanjie Lun, is a classical-Chinese paraphrase: he rendered liberty as “自繇”, elegant and opinionated, excellent for feeling how the concept was first received, misleading as a precise text. What I ended up doing was read a modern Chinese version for the gist, then go back to the English and check chapter I sentence by sentence.
:::

## 4. What I Put to Use

1. **Ask “who was harmed” before “who is right”.** In an argument, split it in two: has anyone’s concrete interest been touched? If not, leave each side its freedom and stop demanding agreement. This alone removes most pointless heat.
2. **Fit a separate gate to “it’s for your own good”.** Whether you are governing or being governed, check what the reason really is: has the other person been damaged, or is it only that “I think he ought to be that way”?
3. **Treat individuality as a trainable capacity.** Mill says imitation needs one faculty, choosing needs the whole set. In daily life that means deliberately making the small decisions only you can make for yourself — what to read, how to spend a day — so the faculties do not fall idle.

## 5. Closing

Finishing On Liberty, I filed it as a “statement of boundary”: it does not tell you how to live, only where the line lies at which others may reach in. Mill’s answer is hard, and for that very reason it works — vague goodwill convinces no one.
