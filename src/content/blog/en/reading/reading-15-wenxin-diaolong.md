## 1. Why I Read It

At the start of spring 2025 I was stuck on an essay I could not finish: material piled up, the sentences smooth, and yet the moment it reached paper it fell apart. A friend said the problem was one of conception, and handed me the Wenxin Diaolong. I had assumed it was an old book of rhetorical tricks; opening it, I found that Liu Xie had set “before you lift the pen” apart as its own chapter, called Shensi.

The Wenxin Diaolong was completed near the end of the Southern Qi. Its author, Liu Xie, spent his youth lodged in a Buddhist monastery and only later took office. Fifty chapters, all of them about “the effort of the mind in writing” — not how to write beautifully, but what actually happens inside you before a piece of writing takes shape.

What changed my view was the opening of Shensi. It offers no formula, only one condition: stillness. The idea was not unique in Southern Qi poetics, but Liu Xie put it first in the whole section on craft, which amounts to saying that the hardest step in writing is not finding words but emptying the mind.

## 2. The Skeleton First

:::info
Ten scrolls, fifty chapters, roughly 37,000 characters in the received text. The upper half is twenty-five chapters, from the Yuandao to the Shuji; the lower half is another twenty-five, from Shensi to the Xuzhi. It is written throughout in parallel prose, all couplets and tonal cadence — at once the finest demonstration by a man writing about writing, and the first obstacle for a reader today. It was put together near the end of the Southern Qi.
:::

I read it in three blocks:

| Section | Chapters | What it settles |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| The hinge of literature | The five chapters from Yuandao to Biansao | Where writing takes its root |
| Genres | The twenty from Mingshi to Shuji | The rules proper to each kind of text |
| Craft and criticism | From Shensi to Chengqi | How a piece is made, and how it is read |

+++primary Expand: how the lower twenty-five chapters divide (my own annotations)
- **The nineteen on craft (Shensi to Zongshu)**: from conception, style and bone down to tonal rules, allusion and word-choice — a whole production line
- **Shensi**: conception. The book’s hinge, about “how to think”, and placed first of all
- **Tixing and Fenggu**: one on how a writer’s temperament fixes his style, one on giving a piece a spine
- **Qingcai and Rongcai**: feeling against wording, and the art of cutting
- **Shenglv, Zhangju, Lici**: polishing at the level of the sentence
- **The five on criticism (Shixu to Chengqi)**: reading a work back into its age, its nature and its author
- **Zhiyin**: how to be a competent reader, one of the book’s closing movements
- **Xuzhi**: the author’s own preface, a reckoning with a lifetime, and the key to the whole
+++

One caution: the commonest mistake is to treat the book as a manual of technique. Its “writing” is far larger than what we call an essay today — scriptures and edicts, riddles and puns all count. Liu Xie wanted one order that could cover every kind of text.

## 3. Passages and Marginalia

### On the title: one line that says what the book is for

> 夫“文心”者，言为文之用心也。
>
> — Wenxin Diaolong, ch. 50, “Xuzhi”
>
> *My own working translation:* “Wenxin” means the effort of the mind in writing.

==Only in the last chapter of the book does it explain its own title.== Liu Xie does not say this is a set of rules for composition; he says it is the *effort of the mind* in writing, and the whole weight falls on “mind”. This is a book about inner work.

My note: to place your most important definition at the end and leave it to the author himself is rare even now. Read the Xuzhi first and you are handed a map drawn by the author’s own hand.

### On conception: what has to happen before the pen moves

> 是以陶钧文思，贵在虚静，疏瀹五藏，澡雪精神。
>
> — ch. 26, “Shensi”
>
> *My own working translation:* In shaping the thought of writing, what matters most is emptiness and stillness: rinse the five organs, scour the spirit clean.

:::success
**“Emptiness and stillness” is not zoning out; it is clearing the site**
“Shaping” is the potter’s wheel; “rinsing” is washing out. Liu Xie is not describing idleness — he is having you remove the stray thoughts, the preconceptions, the deadline-panic first, and leave a patch of ground free inside. Putting this first in the section on craft is his way of announcing: if you cannot write, it is usually not your hand that is at fault.
:::

++着重点++{.dot}The line that follows is stranger still: “In silent concentration the thought reaches a thousand years; in a quiet change of expression the eye travels ten thousand miles.” Going still is precisely what lets you cross a thousand years. Liu Xie’s take on inspiration and today’s “input before output” shake hands across fifteen hundred years.

### On true and false feeling: two roads for a writer

> 昔诗人什篇，为情而造文；辞人赋颂，为文而造情。
>
> — ch. 31, “Qingcai”
>
> *My own working translation:* The poets of the Book of Songs made their writing to carry feeling; the later rhapsodists manufactured feeling to fit writing already made.

==This is the closest thing in the book to a verdict.== In the Odes, feeling comes first and the writing follows; in the later rhapsodies the writing is set out first and a feeling is then procured to fill it.

My note: “manufacturing feeling to fit writing” predicts almost every instance today of writing in order to post. Liu Xie was not against ornament; he was against ornament turned into the goal — work made that way will not even convince its own author.

### On the understanding critic: how hard it is to read well

> 知音其难哉！音实难知，知实难逢，逢其知音，千载其一乎！
>
> — ch. 48, “Zhiyin”
>
> *My own working translation:* How hard it is to find one who understands! The work is hard to know; the knower is hard to meet; and that the two should meet, once in a thousand years.

[危险]{.label .danger}==Three exclamation marks in a row — the most agitated passage in the book.== The work is hard to understand, the reader hard to find, and the two colliding is a once-a-millennium event.

My note: he then offers a method, and it is not mystical — “Only after playing a thousand tunes does one understand sound; only after seeing a thousand swords does one know blades.” Judgment comes from sheer volume of exposure. The line became the plain truth of connoisseurship: taste is not born, it is fed.

### On tradition and change: do not just circle in the yard

> 文律运周，日新其业。变则可久，通则不乏。
>
> — ch. 29, “Tongbian”
>
> *My own working translation:* The law of writing revolves; its work renews each day. To change is to endure; to carry on is never to run dry.

[笔记]{.label .primary}“Carrying on” is keeping the old ground; “changing” is offering something new. Both are required. Liu Xie warns the man who guards a single style: that is circling in the courtyard, not walking ten thousand miles.

My note: this reads as if aimed straight at today. Copy the ancients phrase for phrase, or chase only the new vocabulary with no foundation at all — Liu Xie thinks neither road goes far. His answer is homely: “Look to the present to make the strange, consult the ancients to fix the law.”

:::warning
**The traps of editions and modern translations**
The difficulty of the Wenxin Diaolong comes in two layers. One is the text: the chapter “Yinxiu” survives incomplete, and the received version also carries passages supplied later by hands unknown; their authenticity has long been disputed, so keep that in mind when you reach it. The other is translation: the book is parallel prose, and its couplets and cadence are part of the meaning. A plain colloquial version reads smoothly but files away the comparison that lives inside the antithesis. My habit was to read the main text slowly against the original, look up hard places in Fan Wenlan’s *Wenxin Diaolong zhu*, and use Zhou Zhenfu’s modern translation as a corridor — understand first, then go back and listen to the sound.
:::

## 4. What I Put to Use

1. **Schedule the “emptiness and stillness”.** Do not wait for inspiration to arrive. Before a long piece, keep ten minutes with nothing read and nothing heard, say the problem to yourself once, and only then begin. Liu Xie’s “rinse the five organs” comes down, today, to that one small act.
2. **Split “feeling” from “writing” before you start.** Ask at the top of every piece: is this “writing made to carry feeling”, or “feeling manufactured to fit the writing”? If it is the second, you are not ready to write; go and make the point clear first.
3. **Take Zhiyin as your standard for reading drafts.** “Only after a thousand tunes” is about editing as much as writing: reread your own old work and judgment slowly returns to your hands. The thing to fear in revision is not reluctance to cut; it is being unable to see what is wrong.

## 5. Closing

Closing the book, what stayed with me was not a technique but an order: read the mind, think it clear, then put down the pen. By placing “conception” at the front of the whole theory, Liu Xie concedes something on behalf of every writer — what costs the most was never the sentence, it was the night before the sentence.
