## 1. Why I Read It

The last book, On Liberty, ended on a line: no one may govern you unless you harm another. Closing it, I got stuck — and then, may you govern yourself? So I turned to Dostoevsky. The Brothers Karamazov is a parricide, and he pushes the question of who can stand outside it all the way down.

I had expected a detective novel, and found that the case is not about who did it but about who had a part in it. Three brothers, a father, a bastard son, and a houseful of talkers — every one of them tied to the death by some thread.

What stopped me was a line from the Elder Zosima. He does not console the woman who comes to him; he tells her a truth close to cruelty: love in action is a harsh and dreadful thing compared with love in dreams. The book’s question starts here — if love, freedom and faith are all this hard, how is a person to live at all?

## 2. The Skeleton First

:::info
The Brothers Karamazov was serialised in The Russian Messenger in 1879-1880 and then published as a book; it is Dostoevsky’s last novel. Four parts, twelve books, and an epilogue. It was planned as only the first part of a larger work — the second was never written, the author dying the following year. It is a murder between father and sons, and it reads as a book about freedom, faith and responsibility.
:::

Before beginning I laid out the four parts, so the cast would not swamp me:

| Part | Books | Content | How I read it |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| One | I to III | Family history, a gathering, the sensualists | Get to know the family first |
| Two | IV to VI | Conflict, Ivan’s “Rebellion”, Zosima | The book’s intellectual core is in V and VI |
| Three | VII to IX | The elder’s death, Mitya’s fall, the investigation | Ideas drop back into action and desire |
| Four | X to XII | Boys, Ivan’s collapse, the court | “Who is guilty” is handed to a trial |
| Epilogue | — | Alyosha at the great stone | The close, and the book’s true register |

+++primary Expand: one line per book (standard titles)
- **Book I, The History of a Family**: first, how this family came apart
- **Book II, An Unfortunate Gathering**: the awkward dinner at the monastery, where every conflict is planted
- **Book III, The Sensualists**: Dmitri’s confession; the soliloquy on beauty is here
- **Book IV, Lacerations**: people wounding one another; hardly a clean conversation in it
- **Book V, Pro and Contra**: Ivan’s “Rebellion” and The Grand Inquisitor — the heart of the book
- **Book VI, The Russian Monk**: Zosima’s life and teaching, set face to face with Book V
- **Book VII, Alyosha**: the protagonist finally moves; the most “action” the book has
- **Book VIII, Mitya**: Dmitri’s descent and frenzy — the fastest-written book of them all
- **Book IX, The Preliminary Investigation**: the case laid out, evidence and motive at war
- **Book X, Boys**: a side-track that looks idle, and is really paving the way to the epilogue
- **Book XI, Ivan Fyodorovich**: Ivan’s three meetings with Smerdyakov, the coldest scenes in the novel
- **Book XII, A Judicial Error**: the court convicts a man of murder, and convicts the wrong one
- **Epilogue**: Alyosha speaks to the children at the stone; the last words are “Hurrah for Karamazov”
+++

One caution: do not rush to take sides. The most persuasive speeches in this novel often come from the least trustworthy people. Dostoevsky’s method is not to make you agree with anyone; it is to make you recognise a little of yourself in each of them.

## 3. Passages and Marginalia

### On beauty: the battlefield inside one man

> The awful thing is that beauty is mysterious as well as terrible. God and the devil are fighting there and the battlefield is the heart of man.
>
> — The Brothers Karamazov, Bk III, ch. 3 (trans. Garnett)

==Dmitri says this while confessing that he is pulled by the noble and the base at once.== Dostoevsky does not write “evil” as somebody else’s property; he puts it back inside the person.

My note: the passage is often lifted out as “Dostoevsky on beauty”, when its setting is filthy — the man saying it is sunk in lust and shame. Cut the setting away and the sentence goes weightless.

### On love: a consolation that refuses to console

> I am sorry I can say nothing more consoling to you, for love in action is a harsh and dreadful thing compared with love in dreams.
>
> — The Brothers Karamazov, Bk II, ch. 4 (trans. Garnett)

++着重点++{.dot}The woman who comes for help wants reassurance; Zosima hands her a warning. He splits love in two: the dream kind is self-importance on a stage, the active kind is the daily grind of trying.

My note: the line almost forecasts the ending. What Alyosha does later is small things, one by one — sitting with children, remembering names, keeping his word. He does not shout about love; he simply lets it land on the ground.

### On faith: a man returning his ticket

> And therefore I hasten to return my ticket. [...] It’s not that I don’t accept God, Alyosha, I just most respectfully return him the ticket.
>
> — The Brothers Karamazov, Bk V, ch. 4, “Rebellion” (trans. Garnett)

[危险]{.label .danger}==Ivan is no atheist; what he protests is “that world” itself.== He tells of a child tormented to death, and then says the entrance price is too high, he cannot pay it, he hands the ticket back.

My note: the force of the line lies precisely in that it does not attack God, only refuses the cost. Ivan does not want a rebuttal; he wants the suffering handed back to whoever permits it. Alyosha says only, “That is rebellion,” and lowers his eyes.

### On freedom: a monologue about “miracle, mystery and authority”

> We have corrected Thy work and have founded it upon miracle, mystery and authority.
>
> — The Brothers Karamazov, Bk V, ch. 5, The Grand Inquisitor (trans. Garnett)

:::success
**The Inquisitor’s three words are a list of governing tools**
“Miracle” replaces free choice, “mystery” replaces questioning, “authority” replaces judgement. His argument is that men cannot carry freedom, so the gift you gave was too heavy; we take it back and substitute something easier to manage. Ivan writes the poem not to let the Inquisitor win, but to lay that rebuttal on the table.
:::

My note: this passage is the heart of the book because it writes freedom as a burden rather than a prize. It joins straight onto On Liberty: Mill says others must not govern you; Ivan says men may not dare to take up an ungoverned freedom at all.

### On the ending: the shout by the great stone

> “Hurrah for Karamazov!”
>
> — The Brothers Karamazov, epilogue, the last words of the book (trans. Garnett)

[笔记]{.label .primary}To give the book’s final sentence to a group of children is a heavy choice. After so much squalor, it closes on a company agreeing to “remember this moment”.

My note: what Alyosha gives them is not doctrine but a very concrete proposal — whatever they later become, they must remember this moment they once spent together. The novel starts from a murder and ends on a shared memory, and that is roughly Dostoevsky’s answer: salvation is not elsewhere, it is in the little that people manage to keep between them.

:::warning
**A trap of quotations, and a trap of translations**
First the quotation. One line travels everywhere — “If God does not exist, everything is permitted” — and is usually credited to Dostoevsky. Strictly, it is not a single line in the novel; it is a paraphrase distilled from what Ivan says and what Smerdyakov repeats. Quote it as “the sense of it”, not as the text.
Then the translations. The common Chinese versions are mainly Geng Jizhi’s and Rong Rude’s, and they differ considerably in wording; among English versions Garnett’s is the most widely read, though it is early-twentieth-century English with an old-fashioned sentence. The repetitions and prolixity of the Russian are a deliberate voice, and every translation makes choices for the reader. Best to hold to one version start to finish and not hop between them — voice is the life of this novel.
:::

## 4. What I Put to Use

1. **Sort the names before you read.** Dmitri is Mitya, Alexei is Alyosha, Ivan is Vanya, and the fourth son, Smerdyakov, lives in another position entirely. With the names straight, the long “who is arguing with whom” conversations become readable.
2. **Read Books V and VI together.** Ivan’s “Rebellion” and Zosima’s teaching stand face to face; read one alone and you will think the author takes a side. Hear both and you see his method is to leave the question open.
3. **Keep watching who feels he had a share.** The real movement of the novel is not in the case but in what each person admits: whoever first owns his connection to the death is the one who begins to change.

## 5. Closing

Finishing The Brothers Karamazov, I found my idea of freedom split in two. The last book drew the outer boundary; this one describes the inner struggle — one says no one may govern you, the other says you may not be able to govern yourself. Put together, the conclusion is not comfortable: freedom is both a right against interference and a weight a person must carry alone.

Next: Walden. From the loudest crowd to the quietest pond — I want to see what the same freedom becomes when it is set down beside a man alone.

[^1]: The Brothers Karamazov was planned as only the first part of a much larger work; the second was never written, Dostoevsky dying in 1881. So part of these people’s fate is left hanging forever.
[^2]: The three brothers’ formal names are Dmitri, Ivan and Alexei; “Mitya”, “Vanya” and “Alyosha” are diminutives; the fourth son, Smerdyakov, was born to the father and a wandering woman. Keep the formal name and the diminutive apart and you can tell who is speaking to whom in every scene.
