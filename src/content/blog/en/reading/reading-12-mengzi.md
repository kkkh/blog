## 1. Why Read It

My reason for picking up Mencius was practical. Quoting the Analects in my writing always felt steady and flat; swapping in a single line of Mencius could hold up a whole paragraph. I wanted to know where that push came from, so I started from the beginning.

Halfway through the first pass, I wanted to stop. It is far longer than the Analects, its passages unfold whole, and just as two people are in question and answer, it suddenly veers off into history. What turned it around for me was noticing that almost every line of Mencius lives inside a conversation. He is rarely stating; he is mostly answering.

Reading further, I realised he almost never answers directly. A question is thrown at him, he takes it, and then he sets a question of his own, leading the other person step by step to a position they will admit to. One afternoon I wrote nothing in the margins but one thing: “who is this line spoken to”. After a few pages, tone, heat, and measure all came clear.

## 2. The Skeleton First

:::info
Mencius has seven books — King Hui of Liang, Gongsun Chou, Duke Wen of Teng, Li Lou, Wan Zhang, Gaozi, and Jin Xin — each in two parts, fourteen sections by the old count, some thirty-five thousand characters. Tradition holds that Mencius compiled it with his disciples Wan Zhang, Gongsun Chou and others; Zhu Xi of the Southern Song placed it alongside the Great Learning, the Doctrine of the Mean and the Analects as the “Four Books”, and after that nearly every student had to pass through it[^1]. Compared with the Analects, its passages read more like complete speeches: a scene, an opponent, an exchange back and forth.
:::

So I carry no “book of quotations”, only three maps:

| Approach | What it tracks | What it solves |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| By opponent | Whom he is speaking to | How much force a line carries |
| By analogy | His habit of arguing through beasts, water, farming | Why he circles before making the point |
| By position | Benevolent rule, the goodness of human nature, righteousness against profit | Threading his claims onto one line |

+++primary Expand: how the seven books divide the work (my own annotations)
- **King Hui of Liang**: speaking to rulers, the densest political argument
- **Gongsun Chou**: on qi, on the mind, on the unshaken heart — the most philosophical
- **Duke Wen of Teng**: institutions, the well-field system, funerals — the most like governance
- **Li Lou**: short pieces, aphorisms clustered here
- **Wan Zhang**: historical figures and when to serve or withdraw, the most like character criticism
- **Gaozi**: on human nature, the loudest argument in the book
- **Jin Xin**: words of late life — heaven, fate, the mind — the quietest close
+++

One caution: the commonest mistake is to pull Mencius’s famous lines out of their dialogue and keep them on their own. Those lines are half a move inside an argument; lift one out and the game is gone, leaving only a handsome sentence.

## 3. Passages and Marginalia

### On “arguing”: he says he has no choice

> 予岂好辩哉？予不得已也。
>
> — Mencius, Duke Wen of Teng, Part 2
>
> *My own working translation:* Do I love to argue? I argue because I have no choice.

==These few words are the book’s self-portrait: he knows he looks like a man who quarrels, and he offers a defence for it.== His disciple’s words were “outsiders say you love to argue”; he does not deny the impression, only supplies a reason — the world swings between order and chaos, false teachings rise together, and if he does not stand up and put things plainly, no one will.

My note: I first read Mencius as a hot-tempered man. Only at this line did I see that even the reputation for loving argument had been weighed — he knew how he would look, and spoke up only when there was no way not to. A man who truly loves quarrelling does not write himself a paragraph like this.

### On “cannot” and “won’t”

> 挟太山以超北海，语人曰「我不能」，是诚不能也；为长者折枝，语人曰「我不能」，是不为也，非不能也。
>
> — Mencius, King Hui of Liang, Part 1
>
> *My own working translation:* To tuck Mount Tai under your arm and leap over the North Sea, and tell people “I cannot” — that is truly not being able. To bend for an elder’s sake, and tell people “I cannot” — that is not doing it, not inability[^2].

[笔记]{.label .primary}==This line splits the greatest everyday consolation into two halves: what you truly cannot do, and what you pretend you cannot do.== He says it to King Xuan of Qi: if the king says he cannot “bring kingship to the world”, that is not the Mount Tai kind of thing, it is the bending-for-an-elder kind.

My note: I aimed this at myself for a week. Most of what I had been putting off fell apart under the question — it was not “cannot”, it was “won’t”. This sentence gives no comfort, only a mirror, and the mirror usually shows something not pretty.

### On “analogy”: fifty steps laughing at a hundred

> 王好战，请以战喻。填然鼓之，兵刃既接，弃甲曳兵而走，或百步而后止，或五十步而后止。以五十步笑百步，则何如？
>
> — Mencius, King Hui of Liang, Part 1
>
> *My own working translation:* You love war, so let me use war for the comparison. The drums roll, the blades cross, armour is thrown off and weapons dragged as men run; some stop after a hundred paces, some after fifty. If one who ran fifty paces laughed at one who ran a hundred, what would you say?

==The sharpness is here: he does not tell King Hui that his rule is bad, he makes the king say “bad” himself.== The king answers, “That would not do — it was only not a hundred paces; still he ran”. Mencius replies: if the king understands this, he should stop expecting his own people to outnumber his neighbours.

My note: this is the point in my subtitle. Mencius hardly pours in argument; he builds a scene you cannot refute and lets your answer come out of your own mouth. More than “you ought to”, he trusts “you tell me”.

:::success
**Ask first: “who is he arguing with”**

The most useful single act when reading Mencius is to write at the edge of each passage: whom is this said to. The same line, said to King Hui and said to a disciple, carries nothing alike. Find the opponent and the tone and purpose both rise to the surface.
:::

### On “qi”: where an argument’s backbone comes from

> 我善养吾浩然之气。……其为气也，至大至刚，以直养而无害，则塞于天地之间。
>
> — Mencius, Gongsun Chou, Part 1
>
> *My own working translation:* I am good at nourishing my vast moral qi. This qi is supremely great and supremely firm; nourished in uprightness and left unharmed, it fills the space between heaven and earth.

> 富贵不能淫，贫贱不能移，威武不能屈，此之谓大丈夫。
>
> — Mencius, Duke Wen of Teng, Part 2
>
> *My own working translation:* Riches and honours cannot corrupt him, poverty and low station cannot shift him, power and force cannot bend him — that is what is called a great man.

++着重点++{.dot}The earlier passages all teach how to argue; these two turn back to ask where an argument’s backbone comes from: not technique, but a qi a man has nourished himself. ==The three conditions of the “great man” are all negative — they say what he will not do, not what he will achieve.==

My note: only here did Mencius take hold with me. A man who can only corner others with analogies is a debater; a man who still spends effort saying how that qi is to be nourished is closer to a teacher. What he calls nourishing “in uprightness” cannot be assembled on the spot; it can only be lived honestly day by day.

:::warning
**A line usually read too large**

“The people are the most important; the state is next; the ruler is the least” (Jin Xin, Part 2) travels widely and is often taken for an ancient declaration of democracy. In the original it concerns the order of weight among sacrifices and political power: in Mencius’s frame the people matter most because a state must be founded on people. To read it directly as “popular sovereignty” in the modern sense is to add a layer the sentence does not carry. With lines quoted this often, go back to the question they were meant to answer.
:::

## 4. What I Actually Use

1. **Before reading, write down “who is he arguing with”.** A line whose listener you don’t know is a line not yet read. Once the opponent is written out, you find many passages are answering one particular person, not stating an abstract principle.
2. **When you meet a famous line, read three sentences back.** Restore the context and many “quotable lines” turn back into “one move”. That move, inside the game, usually weighs more than it did when lifted out.
3. **Learn his method, not only his conclusion.** The move that splits “cannot” from “won’t” can be aimed straight at yourself: with something you keep putting off, first ask whether it is true inability or unwillingness.

## 5. Closing

What Mencius taught me comes down to a kind of courtesy in argument: let the other person finish, then hand his own words gently back to him. He is not without anger — the seven books are full of his heat. Yet before those kings who would not listen, he still carried every point to the end, sentence by sentence. That unwillingness to take the shortcut is more memorable than any of his famous lines.

Next: Anna Karenina. Mencius persuades one person at a time; Tolstoy has to mind dozens at once. I want to see how a novel finishes the sentence when the argument steps off the stage and only life is left.

[^1]: On the book’s making: seven books in two parts each, fourteen sections, some thirty-five thousand characters. Unlike the Analects, tradition holds it was compiled by Mencius together with disciples such as Wan Zhang and Gongsun Chou; how much is in his own hand is still debated.

[^2]: Two readings of 折枝: one takes it as breaking off a twig, an utterly easy thing; the other takes 枝 as 肢, massaging an elder’s limbs. Either way the point stands on “something effortless”, which is what makes the difference between “won’t” and “cannot” hold.
